## Problem

Fraud models are easy to train and hard to act on. Fraud is rare here, about 1 in 190 transactions, so a model that never flags anything is 99.5% accurate and useless. Analysts can only check a few cases a day, so the real question isn't "is this fraud?" but "which cases deserve a person's time?"

The usual answer is to review the highest-scoring transactions. That spends review slots on small purchases that happen to look suspicious.

## What I built

A system that turns a fraud probability into one of three actions by comparing expected dollar costs:

- **Approve** costs probability × amount, the fraud you would lose
- **Review** costs $5 of analyst time
- **Block** costs (1 − probability) × $10 for turning away a real customer

Each transaction gets the cheapest action, and each day's 30 review slots go to the cases where a review saves the most. Analysts work through a review app that shows the three reasons behind each score and the cost of each action.

## Results

Tested on three held-out months, October to December 2020: 281,521 transactions, 936 frauds worth $489,894. Every policy gets the same 30 reviews a day.

| Policy | Fraud lost | Reviews | Customers declined | Total cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Approve everything | $489,894 | 0 | 0 | $489,894 |
| Review highest probabilities | $24,164 | 2,760 | 0 | $37,964 |
| Review highest expected dollars | $10,563 | 2,052 | 0 | $20,823 |
| **Cost-based: approve, review or block** | **$4,527** | **1,419** | **114** | **$12,762** |

The model itself reaches a test PR-AUC of 0.838 against 0.376 for a logistic regression baseline, and reviewing the riskiest 1% of transactions catches 93.3% of fraud.

## Key decisions

### Time-based splits only

Train on January 2019 to June 2020, tune on July to August, calibrate on September, test on October to December. A random split would let the model learn from the future.

### Leak-free features, with a test that proves it

Every history window ends one microsecond before the current transaction. A test adds a later transaction and checks that no earlier feature changes.

### Calibrated probabilities

The policy multiplies probability by amount, so probabilities have to match real fraud rates. Training weighted fraud 13 times, which made raw scores about twice too high; Platt scaling on a held-out month fixes that.

> Probability ranking wastes review slots on small purchases. Ranking by expected dollars, with the same budget, cuts fraud lost by more than half.

### Score offline, serve precomputed results

The deployed API never loads LightGBM or SHAP, so it fits on free hosting. A test checks that production mode never imports them.

## Limitations

- No fraud in the dataset is above $1,376, so the model learned that very large purchases are safe. Real fraudsters would exploit that.
- The $5 review and $10 decline costs are assumptions. The conclusion holds when the review cost is halved or doubled and when the decline cost rises to $50 or $100, though blocking becomes much more cautious.
- The data is the public Kaggle Sparkov simulation, not real card data.
